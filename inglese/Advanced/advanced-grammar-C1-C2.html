How to use this reference

At C1–C2 the examiner looks for a range of complex structures used accurately and appropriately — not just correct sentences, but native-like control of emphasis, hypothesis and formal register. This pack gathers the structures that make the difference, grouped into ten themes.

How each entry works

Every entry gives the name of the structure, its form (in the grey pattern box, e.g. Had + S + p.p., … would have + p.p.), a short explanation and two example sentences. An italic gloss such as (= If I had known) shows the plain equivalent.

Register key: formal written/official English   literary very formal or old-fashioned   BrE especially British   spoken conversational
Study tip: don't just recognise these — produce them. Take a plain sentence (If it hadn't rained, we'd have gone) and rewrite it three ways: inversion (Had it not rained…), but for (But for the rain…), and a cleft (It was the rain that stopped us). The ability to paraphrase one idea in several registers is exactly what C2 rewards.

Section 1

The conditional system

Zero conditional
If + present simple, … present simple
General truths, scientific facts and habits — the condition always has the same result.
  • If you heat water to 100°C, it boils.
  • If the alarm goes off, everyone leaves the building.
First conditional
If + present, … will / can / may / imperative
A real, likely possibility in the future and its result.
  • If we leave now, we'll catch the early train.
  • If you see Anna, tell her to call me.
Second conditional
If + past simple, … would / could / might + inf.
An unreal or unlikely present/future situation and its imagined result.
  • If I had more time, I'd learn the piano.
  • If I were you, I'd apologise straight away.
Third conditional
If + past perfect, … would have + p.p.
An unreal past — the opposite of what really happened; often regret or criticism.
  • If she had revised, she would have passed.
  • If we'd left earlier, we wouldn't have missed the flight.
Mixed conditional — past → present
If + past perfect, … would + inf. (now)
A past condition with a present result.
  • If I had accepted that job, I'd be living in London now.
  • If they hadn't sold the house, they'd still be neighbours.
Mixed conditional — present → past
If + past simple, … would have + p.p.
A present/general (unchanging) condition with a past result.
  • If he weren't so careless, he wouldn't have lost the keys.
  • If I spoke Japanese, I'd have taken that posting last year.
Section 2

Beyond “if”: conditional alternatives

unless
“If … not / except if.” Introduces the only thing that would change the result.
  • You won't pass unless you revise. (= if you don't revise)
  • I'll be there by six unless the train is delayed.
provided / providing (that) · as long as · on condition that
A strong condition — “only if this is true.” On condition that is the most formal.
  • I'll lend it to you provided (that) you return it by Friday.
  • He was released on condition that he report to the police weekly.
supposing / suppose · what ifspoken
Introduces a hypothesis, usually in conversation; often followed by a second conditional.
  • Supposing you lost your job, what would you do?
  • What if we're wrong about him?
in case
A precaution: you do X because Y might happen — not the same as if.
  • Take an umbrella in case it rains. (take it now, as a precaution)
  • I'll write it down in case I forget.
otherwise / or (else)
“If not / if you don't” — states the negative consequence.
  • Write it down; otherwise you'll forget.
  • We must leave now, or else we'll be late.
but for · if it weren't / hadn't been forformal
“Without (this), the result would be different.” But for is followed by a noun.
  • But for your help, I'd have failed. (= if it hadn't been for your help)
  • If it weren't for the noise, this flat would be perfect.
even if
The result stays the same whether or not the condition is true (concession).
  • Even if we ran, we wouldn't make it now.
  • I wouldn't sign it even if they doubled the offer.
Section 3

Conditional inversion (formal, without “if”)

Were + subjectformal
Were + S (…), … would + inf.
Formal equivalent of the second conditional; drop if and put were first.
  • Were I in your position, I'd resign. (= If I were…)
  • Were she a little older, she'd understand.
Were + subject + to-infinitiveformal
Were + S + to-inf., … would + inf.
A hypothetical future possibility — common in careful or diplomatic writing.
  • Were the government to fall, elections would follow.
  • Were you to reconsider, we'd gladly welcome you back.
Were it not for + nounformal
Present “without.” Inverted form of if it weren't for.
  • Were it not for your support, I'd give up. (= If it weren't for…)
Had + subject + past participleformal
Had + S + p.p., … would have + p.p.
Formal equivalent of the third conditional; drop if and put had first.
  • Had I known, I would have told you. (= If I had known…)
  • Had they invested sooner, they'd have doubled their money.
Had it not been for + nounformal
Past “without.” Inverted form of if it hadn't been for.
  • Had it not been for the storm, we'd have arrived on time.
Should + subjectformal
Should + S + inf., … (imperative / will)
A formal first conditional suggesting the event is possible but not expected — common in correspondence and instructions.
  • Should you require assistance, please call reception. (= If you (should) need…)
  • Should anything go wrong, contact us immediately.
Section 4

Inversion after negative & limiting adverbials

Never / Not once / At no time
Negative adverbial + auxiliary + S + verb
Front a negative adverbial for emphasis and invert like a question.
  • Never had I seen such chaos.
  • Not once did she complain.
Rarely / Seldom / Little
Little is fixed with verbs of knowing/realising (= not at all).
  • Seldom do we see such talent.
  • Little did they know what awaited them.
Hardly / Scarcely … when · No sooner … than
Two things happening in quick succession. Note when vs than; both use inversion + past perfect.
  • Hardly had we sat down when the phone rang.
  • No sooner had I left than it started to rain.
Not only … but (also)
Adds a second, stronger point; invert after not only.
  • Not only did he lie, but he also blamed his colleague.
  • Not only is she brilliant, she's also generous.
Not until / Only when / Only after / Only then
Inversion falls on the main clause, not the time clause.
  • Not until midnight did they finish.
  • Only when the lights came on did we realise the danger.
Only by / Only in this way
Emphasises the sole means of achieving something.
  • Only by working together can we succeed.
  • Only in this way will the problem be solved.
Under no circumstances / On no accountformal
A strong prohibition; often paired with be to or a modal.
  • Under no circumstances are you to open that door.
  • On no account should the documents be copied.
So + adj. … (that) · Such … (that)
Front so + adjective or such + noun for dramatic emphasis.
  • So great was the demand that tickets sold out in minutes.
  • Such was her charm that everyone forgave her.
Section 5

The subjunctive & the unreal past

Mandative (present) subjunctiveformal
suggest / insist / demand / recommend / propose / essential + that + S + base form
The base form for every person (no -s, no was/is); negative = not + base.
  • The board recommended that he resign.
  • It is essential that every member be present, and that latecomers not be admitted.
“should” alternativeBrE
British English often uses should + base form for the same meaning — slightly less formal.
  • The board recommended that he should resign.
  • I insist that you should be told the truth.
were-subjunctive (unreal)
Use were for all persons after if, wish, as if/as though, suppose, would rather.
  • If I were you… / She talks as if she were the boss.
  • I wish it were Friday.
wish / if only + past
Regret about the present. If only is more emphatic than wish.
  • I wish I knew the answer.
  • If only he were here now.
wish / if only + past perfect
Regret about the past — something you can no longer change.
  • I wish I had listened to your advice.
  • If only we hadn't sold the flat.
wish + would
Annoyance or a desire for change in someone else's behaviour (not your own).
  • I wish you would stop interrupting.
  • I wish it would stop raining.
It's (high / about) time + past
Says something is overdue; the past form has present meaning.
  • It's high time we left.
  • It's about time you learned to drive.
would rather / would sooner + past
Preference about another person's action; use a past form for present/future.
  • I'd rather you didn't smoke in here.
  • She'd sooner we came on Sunday.
Fixed subjunctive expressionsliterary
Survivals of the subjunctive kept as set phrases.
  • Be that as it may… / Come what may… / So be it.
  • Suffice (it) to say… / Far be it from me to criticise… / Long live the King.
lestliterary
“In order that … not / for fear that,” followed by a subjunctive or should.
  • He spoke quietly lest he wake the baby.
  • They kept watch lest anyone should escape.
Section 6

Cleft sentences & emphasis

It-cleft
It + be + focus + who / that …
Splits a sentence to spotlight one element (person, thing, time, reason).
  • It was Maria who solved the problem. (not anyone else)
  • It wasn't until 1990 that the truth emerged.
Wh-cleft (pseudo-cleft)
What + clause + be + focus
Fronts a what-clause to emphasise an action or thing.
  • What I need is a proper holiday.
  • What she did was (to) resign on the spot.
The thing / reason / place … is
A flexible cleft using a general noun as the launch-pad.
  • The reason (why) I called is to apologise.
  • The thing that annoys me is the waste.
All (that) … is
Means “the only thing”; downplays or limits.
  • All I want is a quiet life.
  • All you have to do is sign here.
Fronting for emphasis
Move an object or complement to the front for contrast or drama.
  • That kind of rudeness I simply won't tolerate.
  • Really impressive, the whole performance was.
Emphatic do / does / did
Add the auxiliary to stress a positive verb or to contradict.
  • I do apologise for the delay.
  • She did warn you — you just didn't listen.
Section 7

Participle & absolute clauses

Present participle (-ing) clause
Same subject as the main clause; expresses time, reason or a simultaneous action.
  • Feeling tired, she went straight to bed.
  • Living abroad, he rarely sees his family.
Perfect participle (Having + p.p.)
Shows the first action is clearly completed before the second.
  • Having finished the report, he logged off.
  • Having lived there for years, she knew every street.
Past participle (-ed) clause
Has a passive meaning (= because/if it is/was …).
  • Shocked by the news, they fell silent.
  • If treated early, the condition is curable.
Absolute constructionformal
noun + participle, … main clause
A clause with its own subject and no conjunction — very formal/written.
  • The weather being fine, we set off at dawn.
  • Her work finished, she headed home.
Fixed absolute phrases
Common set expressions built on the same pattern.
  • All things considered, it went well. / Weather permitting, we'll walk.
  • That said, I take your point. / Present company excepted.
Reduced relative clause
A participle replaces who/which + be, tightening the sentence.
  • The man standing by the door… (= who is standing)
  • Documents signed today take effect at once. (= that are signed)
Section 8

The advanced & impersonal passive

Reporting-verb passive (personal)formal
S + be + said / believed / thought + to-inf. / to have + p.p.
Reports opinion or rumour impersonally; use to have + p.p. for earlier time.
  • He is said to be extremely wealthy.
  • She is believed to have left the country.
Impersonal “It is … that”formal
The other way to report a general view — common in news and academic writing.
  • It is thought that the talks will resume shortly.
  • It was once believed that the earth was flat.
Causative: have something done
have + object + past participle
You arrange for someone else to do a service for you.
  • We're having the kitchen renovated.
  • You should have your eyes tested.
Experiential have / get something done
Describes something (often unpleasant) that happened to you. Get is more informal.
  • He had his wallet stolen on the train.
  • I got my hair cut yesterday.
need / want + -ingBrE
A passive meaning without a passive form (= needs to be done).
  • The car needs washing.
  • This shirt wants ironing.
get-passivespoken
An informal alternative to the be-passive, often for sudden or unwelcome events.
  • She got promoted last month.
  • He got caught cheating.
Section 9

Advanced modality

must / can't (couldn't) have + p.p.
Near-certain deduction about the past: must = surely true, can't = surely false.
  • She must have left already — her coat's gone.
  • He can't have known; nobody told him.
might / may / could have + p.p.
Past possibility — one option among several.
  • They might have missed the turning.
  • It could have been worse.
should / ought to have + p.p.
An expectation that wasn't met — criticism or regret.
  • You should have told me sooner.
  • I ought to have known better.
needn't have vs didn't need to
Needn't have + p.p. = you did it but it was unnecessary; didn't need to = it wasn't necessary (often not done).
  • You needn't have paid — it was free. (but you did pay)
  • I didn't need to pay, so I kept the cash.
would / used to (past habits)
Repeated past actions. Use used to (not would) for past states.
  • As a boy he would spend hours reading.
  • We used to live by the sea. (state — not “would live”)
will / would (characteristic behaviour)
Typical or predictable behaviour; stressed will/would shows irritation.
  • She'll sit there reading for hours.
  • He would leave the door open, wouldn't he!
may / might (very) well · may as well
May well = quite likely; may/might as well = there's no better option.
  • You may well be right.
  • We might as well walk — it's quicker than waiting.
Section 10

Formal connectors & register

Concession: albeit · notwithstanding · much as · grantedformal
Formal ways to say “although.” Albeit introduces a phrase, not a full clause.
  • The plan, albeit costly, is sound.
  • Much as I admire her, I can't agree. / Notwithstanding the delay, we finished.
whilst · whereasBrE
Whilst is a formal BrE variant of while; whereas marks direct contrast.
  • Whilst I understand your concern, the rules are clear.
  • He is cautious, whereas she takes risks.
Consequence: hence · thus · thereby · accordinglyformal
Formal links of result. Thereby + -ing shows a resulting effect.
  • He missed the deadline; hence the penalty.
  • She signed early, thereby avoiding the queue.
whereby · insofar as · thereof · herebyformal
Legal/official connectors: whereby = by which; insofar as = to the extent that.
  • a scheme whereby costs are shared.
  • insofar as the law allows / I hereby declare the meeting open.
be to + infinitiveformal
Official future plans, instructions or orders — common in news and rules.
  • The Prime Minister is to visit Rome next week.
  • Staff are to report to reception on arrival.
Register shifts: neutral → formal
Swap everyday words for their formal equivalents in essays and letters.
  • get → obtain/receive · ask for → request · need → require · enough → sufficient.
  • but → however · also → moreover/furthermore · so → therefore · a lot of → a great deal of.
